● Here's Haily's take on Pride and Prejudice 🫖

  Pride and Prejudice: A Companion's Guide

  The Twin Themes That Name the Book

  Austen puts her two central vices right in the title. Pride and prejudice
  aren't just flaws — they're a knot both protagonists must work loose. Darcy
  embodies pride (wealth and consequence making him cold), but Elizabeth has her
  own pride — in her perceptiveness — and that's what trips her up. Her
  prejudice springs from wounded vanity (Darcy's slight at the Meryton ball) and
  is fed by Wickham's lies. The genius: neither flaw belongs cleanly to one
  person. They're mirrors, which is why they can grow toward each other.

  Marriage and Social Class

  The engine of the plot. Austen lays out a full spectrum:
  - Charlotte & Mr. Collins — pure pragmatism ("I am not romantic, you know").
  - Lydia & Wickham — impulse and lust, a cautionary tale.
  - The Bennets — attraction curdled into mismatched temperaments.
  - Elizabeth & Darcy / Jane & Bingley — the ideal: love, mutual respect, and
  compatible standing.

  Austen knows money matters, but insists the best marriages need affection and
  esteem on top of it.

  Reputation & Individual vs. Society

  Lydia's elopement threatens to sink the whole family — one scandal, all five
  sisters ruined. Yet Austen champions the individual: Elizabeth refuses to
  bend, most gloriously against Lady Catherine. Judging people by character
  rather than rank is quietly radical.

  Self-Knowledge and Growth

  The warm heart of the book. Elizabeth's turning point — Darcy's letter: "Till 
  this moment, I never knew myself." Darcy's growth is subtler, proven through
  action (rescuing Lydia) rather than words. They become better people first —
  the romance is earned.

  The Two Leads

  Elizabeth — witty, independent, flawed, self-aware enough to grow; wrong about
  important things, which makes her real. Darcy — the template for a thousand
  brooding heroes, but his arc (learning humility undermined his real virtues)
  is more interesting than his imitators.

  Austen's Style — the real magic

  - Irony — that opening line sounds like wisdom; it's a sly joke (the mothers
  want the wife-hunting).
  - Free indirect discourse — we slip in and out of Elizabeth's head, so we're
  fooled about Darcy and Wickham right alongside her. The reader is made
  complicit.
  - Social satire — Mr. Collins's groveling, Mrs. Bennet's nerves, Lady
  Catherine's imperiousness — devastatingly accurate, never cruel.

  Takeaway

  It works on every level at once: swoony romance, sharp social comedy, and a
  serious meditation on how hard — and how worthwhile — it is to know ourselves
  and see others clearly.
